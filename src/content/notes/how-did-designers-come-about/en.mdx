<p class="loud">Part 1. Reality and statistics</p>

We’ll talk about this because every month tens of thousands of people google:

- How do I become a designer?
- Where should I study?
- How much can I earn?
- What fields are there?

About 2,500 designers are studying at HSE Design School right now. Just imagine the competition for every place. Every big YouTube channel is stuffed with ads for design courses. If you’re reading this, you’re most likely a designer too. Why, now, in 2025, as I write this article, does everyone want to become a designer?

There’s always a basic answer to questions like this: “Well, of course, it’s all technological progress. That’s just how the world is. That’s it.” But what is this world like? Technological progress has already put elevator operators, rag-and-bone men, scribes and log drivers out of work. Thousands of people. Why did it take pity on designers in particular?

Guys, it seems we did something unique for this world. And I suggest we figure it out.

<div class="ids__space S" />

## Disclaimer

This isn’t another lecture on the history of design — it fills a gap. I studied design at university, so for 3 years I watched and read lectures on design history several times a week. With all the richness of that material, there was no answer to the two main questions:

- *When did designers become in demand?*
- *Why did such huge demand appear?*

![](./how-did-designers-come-about-1.avif)

You won’t read the entire history of design here. We’ll hardly talk about styles, key designers, landmark works or art. Instead, we’ll look at one specific period in the development of design. And not in a vacuum, but together with the economy, wars, propaganda and industry of the time. In short, with everything that usually stays outside the brackets in classic “nice” design lectures.

Get your full range of feelings ready: an ambiguous story is about to begin, one that still affects each of us.

<p class="loud">Part 2. Steam engines and roses</p>

First, let’s figure out when people noticed that design existed. How did it happen? What problems did the first designers face?

<div class="ids__space S" />

## What the world was like

In the 18th, 19th and 20th centuries, three significant events let design take off like a rocket in the 21st century. A chain of events turned craftsmen in little shops into the biggest rock stars in the world.

**The first event: Wedgwood invents the catalog.** Trade is growing. Shops get display windows. Anyone can come in, choose and buy a ready-made item — instead of finding a craftsman, ordering a piece to their own specifications, waiting a long time and praying that it all turns out well.

Things made thousands of kilometers away become available. Choice appears. Now anyone can pick a teacup, a vase and gloves. Beauty becomes accessible to more people.

Now every European can endlessly choose interior items: porcelain, glass and textiles. This fuels their interest in decorating the space where they live and work themselves. Changing your house is harder than rehanging the curtains and getting a new lamp and kettle.

In 1774, the first catalog of ready-made porcelain tableware appeared. Wedgwood’s pottery launched it. Before that, every cup and plate was ordered separately, and every set was unique. Wedgwood cut down the variety of tableware and kept only the best-selling sets in the catalog — sets that, on top of that, were associated with something royal and luxurious.

Catalogs also brought a lot of new difficulties. For example, now every teacup had to be a perfect copy of its picture in the catalog. There were more defective items.

**The second event: the first exhibition of design and technology.** In 1851, the first great exhibition of design and technology took place in England. Guests and exhibits came from 18 countries on every continent. It was the first time in history that 6 million people gathered to discuss inventions.

The picture at the exhibition was roughly this. All around are modern machine tools that nobody could have imagined a couple of decades earlier. Exhibits everywhere, some of them 3–4 times taller than a person. People look at and discuss shiny black machines. Everything is new and high-tech. Now we’ll all live together in a future we couldn’t even dream of yesterday…

And the furniture and plates stand there just like 100 years ago. In curlicues, gilding and roses. Just like at your grandparents’ place. When the exhibition ended, its organizers, Prince Albert and Henry Cole, were shocked by this difference. Something had to change, urgently.

**The third event: Coco Chanel invents prêt-à-porter.** In 1929, Coco Chanel invents a new way of producing clothes. She called it prêt-à-porter. It sounds like something haute couture, but in fact, with 100% probability, you’re wearing clothes like that while reading this article. Tailors developed a few universal sizes: S, M, L, XL. Every collection was presented in these basic sizes. There was no longer any need to make an outfit to individual measurements every time.

Chanel turned design into a business. From here on, this is the only approach to design we’ll meet throughout the whole story. Get your fork ready — we’ve got to the meat.

#### The first reason. A new way of making and buying things

People started buying goods in a new way. Before, a person went to a specific craftsman, and the craftsman created the design directly for the customer.

Now a group of anonymous workers creates a design based on some average idea of the consumer. A person looks at a shop window and chooses from what’s there.

The new way of making and buying goods turns the designer into the main player on the market.

<p class="loud">Part 3. Power and alt girls</p>

Since designers are now getting so much power, we need to meet the people who got it. Let’s go back to the Great Exhibition of design and technology. People had noticed design.

Broadly, in England there were two groups and four main characters. All these men had an illegitimate child. Today we call it Art Nouveau.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## The first group: the state

**How it appeared.** Two years after that very exhibition, Prince Albert and Henry Cole founded the Department of Science and Art. The department then took over the state school of art — the main place in the country that set all the trends.

**The main figure.** Christopher Dresser worked at this school. Christopher, Albert and Cole worked together really productively. First, he immediately hit it off with the management. Second, he really was talented. This genius was 50 years ahead of the rest of the design world. Literally: if you look at his work, you’d think it was the Bauhaus of the 1930s and 40s. But in fact it’s one man who worked at the end of the 19th century.

**Principles.** Christopher Dresser was inspired by two things. Japan, with its functional minimalism. And botany, with its logic and drive to discard everything unnecessary. In botany, people drew diagrams of plants and of the processes going on inside them. This idea of “discarding the unnecessary” appeared right then; later Malevich, the minimalists and even the Bauhaus would remember it.

## The second group: the Pre-Raphaelites

**How it appeared.** There was also an opposition group of designers. It was a less formal society: just designers and artists with similar views on life who met up and discussed the doom of humanity. The Pre-Raphaelite movement developed in the second half of the 19th century. They fought against the conventions of the new ethics, academic traditions and blind imitation of classical models — that is, against everything the first group of designers did.

<div class="ids__space M" />

**The main figure.** The key figure there was William Morris, the first person to call himself a designer at all. In 1883, when joining a political organization in England, he wrote “designer” in the “profession” field.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-32.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={29} />

<div class="ids__space M" />

**Principles.** The Pre-Raphaelites rejected what would later let all designers live and work: industry, progress and machines. They believed that technological progress kills everything human in us, so we, as designers, should never make friends with it. Instead, we need handmade manufactories and brotherly workshops. In short, they wanted everything just like in the Middle Ages, which they romanticized.

## Differences and similarities

Let’s sum up.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-36.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={33} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Here’s what I want to stress in this part. Both groups still made all their work by hand. They depended on the material they worked with, because the material still defined a craftsman’s specialty. However cool the machines and machine tools were, they were needed for factories and heavy industry. Their main job was to build equipment to defend the country, not to make plates. People’s everyday life still depended on other people in small manufactories: tailors, carvers, glassblowers, tanners, potters. Let’s note that…

<div class="ids__space M" />

<div class="card">

#### The second reason. People were embarrassed by design that lagged behind

The Industrial Revolution sped up the pace of life. Thanks to technological progress, people believed for the first time that the future could be better than the past. Design couldn’t keep up.

People believed they were living in the future. Cars and steam trains were already on the streets. The whole city was built up with plants and factories.

This strengthened the belief even more that the bright future had already arrived. And how much confusion those gilded roses caused in this way of life. Society noticed this lame dog. Nobody knew what to do with it yet, but they felt sorry for it.

</div>

<div class="ids__space XL" />

<p class="loud">Part 4. America and gold</p>

Imagine the time of the First World War. The countries of the world are tacitly divided. The principle of division is how much a country’s economy influences world trade — that is, how many goods a country can offer for sale and how cheaply. The terms “Great” and “catching-up” economies appeared. The Great economies were Great Britain, Germany, Russia, France and the USA.

America was the only country with a Great economy that took part in the war indirectly and pragmatically, and so it managed to make money. There were 2 main sources of money: exports and loans. Since the fighting took place in Europe, America could calmly produce goods and supply them to its allies without fear of bombing or looted factories.

Great Britain, France and Russia bought goods from America and took out loans. They bought cotton, wheat, copper, rubber, cars, engineering products and thousands of other raw materials and finished goods. During the war years, the total value of American exports grew almost 3 times: from $2.4 billion in 1913 to $6.2 billion in 1917. The American economy got a huge boost. Unemployment fell. Businesses got bigger markets.

## Gold

And the government managed to cover itself too. During the war years, they accepted payment only in gold. “It’s not that we don’t trust your European currencies, but please finish your business first, and then we’ll talk. In the meantime, we can offer you to pay us in gold, or to bring yours over and keep it in our banks. It’s safe here.”

And it worked. During the First World War, American gold reserves grew 3 times. They owned about 6,500 tons of gold — 40% of the world’s reserves.

Why does the amount of gold affect the economy so much? The gold standard does its job. It means every dollar is backed by a specific piece of gold lying in your country’s safe. If there’s $1,000,000,000 worth of gold in the safe, then $1,000,000,000 worth of bills, coins and securities circulate in the country. You can’t just print another million or two. But if you have 40% of all the world’s gold, you don’t need to. Money to burn.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Boom, jazz, loans

America changed before our eyes!

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-40.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={35} />

<div class="ids__space M" />

First, the boom began. In New York, on Manhattan, skyscrapers went up that were meant to become the largest business centers in the world. There were bars on every street. Jazz played everywhere. Beautiful girls in short skirts with short haircuts danced the tango with equally handsome guys in expensive suits. Every day was a party.

Second, since there’s a lot of money, you can give people loans at low rates. Here’s how it works: the basic rule of economics is “the more of a resource there is, the cheaper it is”. Money is a resource just like wood. So when a country has a lot of free money, interest rates on loans and mortgages go down, because money itself gets cheaper. I think over the last 3 years you’ve definitely noticed the interest rates on loans and mortgages in Russia. You can’t unsee that. It works on the same principle — it’s just that in that time and in that country, money is expensive.

Back to America: that’s how living on credit appeared. Which means ordinary people could now afford things much more expensive than they could really afford.

## Factories work at full capacity

Third, there were more jobs, and production was growing. There was demand for goods, which helped raise wages to a decent level, which created even more jobs. All of Europe needed feeding. At a time like this there’s a big incentive not just to fulfill the work plan, but also to pull old drawings out of the drawers, propose new ideas and refine old ones. That’s exactly what designers and engineers did. So besides weapons and military equipment, knick-knack goods started rolling off the conveyor: mixers, blenders, kettles, small economical planes for travel.

#### The third reason: during the war, a lot of everyday technology reached ordinary buyers

During the war, and thanks to it, one significant thing happened. One more point for the ambiguity of our world :) Plenty of small inventions had been refined well enough. People had also learned to make them simpler and cheaper. They no longer blew up in your hands and could be produced quite cheaply. So they could be sent to store shelves. We’re talking about radio, commercial aviation, refrigerators, ovens, blenders, cameras, vacuum cleaners.

Yes, these are small changes. What can you expect from an ordinary mixer? It’s not a revolution. The real revolution was the volume and scale of production. All sorts of devices tried to replace people’s little chores, without even asking permission. Factories figured out that they could sell a lot of small, cheap items. And if you do that many times, you get good money.

</div>

<div class="ids__space M" />

But here a conflict of interest arises: businesses churned out cheap stuff, and people don’t quite understand why they should buy it. Yes, there are loans, there are goods. But why do I need a mixer if I’ve whisked eggs with a fork all my life and it was fine? Businesses weren’t happy with that answer — you want to smack the person on the back of the head and say “Stop fucking with my head and buy while you can!”. A gentler solution had to be found.

<div class="ids__space XL" />

<p class="loud">Part 5. Cars and uniqueness</p>

We could talk about furniture, clothes, food. About anything, really! Why cars?

First, in the 20th century cars became the main engine of progress. They influenced engineers, who were constantly inventing and modernizing something inside them. Society, thanks to how widespread they were and the hype around them. And the state, thanks to the hidden development of the military-industrial complex.

Second, they’re profitable to develop, and there’s a lot of attention around them. Multiply attention by profit and you get money. And where there’s more money, there’s competition and demand. So selling 1,000 cars is not the same as selling 1,000 mixers.

All of this makes the car market an indicator that is highly sensitive to the changes happening in society. Which means it’s especially important for us to see what they came up with there.

Through the previous parts you’ve surely soaked up the vibe of those years. We’ve already talked about the world as a whole and come down to the level of society and everyday life. Now I suggest going one level of detail deeper and talking about the fate of one car company and a specific person inside it.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## The odd one out

Let’s talk about General Motors. From the very beginning, GM was created purely as a money company. Yes, cars mattered to them, but more as a fast-growing market where you could make money. GM is a business that was created for money from the start. Nothing personal.

It was an unexpected strategy, and only GM managed to pull it off. Because other car companies made money through new developments. Their engineers worked their fingers to the bone: they created new models, improved technology, increased safety, cut costs, sped up assembly lines and endlessly tested new ideas — and that’s how they scraped something together.

Ha, GM didn’t get its hands dirty. They simply bought whole ready-made companies that had already done all the dirty work. For example, they owned the top brands Cadillac and Chevrolet and the less famous Olds Motor Vehicle and Oakland Motor Car. In the first 10 years, from 1908 to 1918, GM bought 30 companies. They got their market share through the volume of companies, plants and jobs rather than through any real contribution to engineering.

## The balance of power

There is, however, one unpleasant fact — inconvenient for GM. It has to do with the best-selling car in America… First, it already existed. Second, it didn’t belong to them. Because it was the 1908 Ford Model T. Ford was GM’s main competitor. Yes, Ford didn’t have that much money, people and production. But it had something more important — passion. For Henry Ford, the car was a philosophy, a wife and the passion of his life.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-51.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={46} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

To make things worse, this car was built under Henry Ford’s motto: “We want the man who buys one of our cars never to have to buy another. We never make an improvement that renders any previous model obsolete.”

A good philosophy. All the companies of the 20th century were built and grew on this principle. It’s still relevant today.

And with this philosophy, Ford was the undisputed market leader — even though they had only one car for sale. Imagine you walk into a shop where there is only one product on the counter. And it’s the Ford Model T. And 60% of all cars sold in the country were this “only product on the counter”.

It turned out this way because Ford considered price the main thing. Remember, business was only a way for him to realize his philosophy. And the philosophy was to let every person feel the thrill of a car that Ford himself felt. So he tried to make the car cheaper through new developments and by cutting existing costs. To make the car as affordable as possible.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-52.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={47} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

At this point you’re probably wondering: where is GM with its endless catalog of acquired companies? What share of the market does such a rich and big company hold? 20%? 30%? The remaining 50%? Almost… 12%. With all its acquired brands, GM owned only 12% of the market. Against Ford’s 60%.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-48.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={48} />

<div class="ids__space M" />

## The boiling point is approaching

Besides GM’s own loss in market share, another big problem was coming for all car companies at once. Car sales started to fall.

On the one hand, by the 1920s, 50% of people already had their own cars. Those who wanted one. Those who could afford one. And those who couldn’t, too — remember the loans. All the “warm” customers already owned a car. They had to move into the “cold” part of the market. That’s harder: slower, more expensive, less predictable.

On the other hand, more new cars were being produced than old ones were breaking down. And while your car isn’t broken, why buy a new one? Remember that thanks to the First World War, people had learned to make machines that lasted for years. Plus there was Ford with his philosophy of durability. So cars didn’t break down, and people kept driving them for years.

And you still want to make new money. It’s capitalism outside. Every month the market shrinks and competition grows. There’s still money to fix everything. We need a good idea to spend that money on. Somehow we have to make people buy a new car even if the old one still works. And looks nice parked in the yard.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## The most important decision in the history of designers

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-50.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={49} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

It was made at GM, and the man behind it was Alfred Sloan. He joined the company in 1923 as a strategist and manager.

Alfred Sloan proposed a new strategy for the company. Looking around at what the neighbors were doing, he did literally the opposite. He believed that the price of a car really mattered. Just like Henry Ford did. But not in terms of affordability and endless price-cutting — in terms of a new mechanism for manipulating people. This will be our salvation.

Alfred Sloan proposed turning the car showroom into society’s social ladder. GM should offer at least 5 different models at the same time. And they should differ not in inventions and gears inside — that’s secondary — but above all in price. So there had to be models for every segment of the market, from an office clerk to a company director. The car became proof of a person’s status and their rise up the career ladder.

He was the first to do what today we would call branding and positioning. Although those words didn’t exist back then.

Developing his strategy further, Alfred Sloan made one more decision. From 1924, new cars were released not because of improved technology, optimization or better safety, but for the sake of a new car design. After all, repainting the same car and drawing a new body shape is easier. Remember, GM didn’t get its hands dirty.

Investing in development is expensive and risky. Because it’s hard to plan how successful a new invention will be in society. And to get 1 good solution, you have to try and bury 10 others. And you still have to pay your workers for all 11.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-55.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={50} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Now cars in all sorts of colors started rolling off the conveyor. What’s more, every year a lineup of cars came out in new colors, so that last year’s colors would look outdated.

An open confrontation begins. Henry Ford says: “Any customer can have a car painted any color that he wants so long as it is black.” And Sloan calls for making the design of new cars so varied, colorful and attractive that it instantly makes people dissatisfied with the old models.

That way you could recognize last year’s — and therefore outdated — model in a second, along with the loser behind the wheel. Driving last year’s car is like walking around with an iPhone 12 in 2025 (I’m talking about myself). Agree? Recognize it? Today the whole world works on this system, and we’ve stopped noticing it. It’s called “planned obsolescence”, and it appeared in 1924.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Off it went

How did GM start carrying out Alfred Sloan’s plan? In 1925, Harley Earl opened the “Art and Color Section” at GM. He invited the 50 most proactive employees there. Their job was to choose colors, invent new squiggles and see what else in a car could be made “prettier”. Yes, this was the first recorded design department in history. And those 50 most proactive employees became the first designers at General Motors.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-53.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={51} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

In the first 5 years of this department, the number of designers doubled. By 1929, more than 100 people worked there. Yes, 100 people sitting in a separate little room choosing between “sea wave” and “turquoise”. At a time when Ford wasn’t even thinking about it.

All the other companies sniffed this out — and not only car companies — and started hiring designers too. Planned obsolescence quickly showed good results in sales. The guys with money started to believe in designers. Like it or not, in a job market like that you, as a designer, would start believing in yourself too.

<div class="ids__space M" />

<div class="card">

#### The fourth reason: planned obsolescence appeared

Every year society wants something new. And companies will offer it and meet the demand. So you always have to make new things and remind people to throw out the old ones. People will always have work, because someone will always have to stand on the production line.

Planned obsolescence can be physical: a glass phone gets scratched, faux-leather shoes wear out, a synthetic T-shirt pills. Or psychological: pressure on our desire to fit in with our own crowd.

Design became the perfect tool, because it solves both problems at once. It helps engineer profitable things and puts pressure on human dignity.

</div>

## More gold!

Remember, it’s still the Roaring Twenties. Abundance! Wealth! Permissiveness! For decades the whole country has been living large. In 1926, 90% of all cars were bought in installments. All the problems are far away — on the other side of the globe, to be precise. If you’d been around 30 at the time, you would not only have had a blast yourself but also raised your kids with a silver spoon in their mouths. Never mind that this life is on credit. The rates are low and there’s much more money. We’ll figure it out.

Then something happened that nobody was ready for.

## What happened

We’re getting into a complicated topic. There will be a lot of simplifications — they’re needed to tell this story quickly. After all, our article is about design, and now it’s going to be about economics and politics. Kisses to everyone who already knows the topic in more depth — props for your erudition.

Let’s start with a simple situation. You have a neighbor, John. A couple of months ago he bought some piece of paper at the bank — a share or a bond, you’ve already forgotten. He spent $10 on it. And today he comes over and says his $10 have turned into $30, because the price of the paper went up. Now he can sell it for more and get $30. Or he can hold on to it and earn even more later if the price goes up again. Completely legal. At an ordinary bank. Once you’ve come to your senses, what do you do? You buy the same piece of paper.

About half the country was like you and your neighbor John. In the 1920s everyone bought stocks and bonds, even if they didn’t work at a bank and didn’t fully understand what they were. Because ads for these stocks and bonds hung on billboards in every city in the country. And the buying process itself was simple: you go to the bank and hand over your money. In short, a mass hysteria on the level of the 21st-century get-rich-quick course business.

It was a time of euphoria. Everyone believed that America was a country that had overcome every hardship, so from here on things would only get better. American society would get richer every day. Prices would always be higher tomorrow than today. So you had to buy as many pieces of paper at the bank as possible.

  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-58.avif'], size: 'XL' },

## The first bubble of the 20th century

Now let’s look at the same situation from the bank’s side. What would you do when the line at your door grows every day? You’d try selling your pieces of paper for $15 instead of $10. That way you earn a bit more. And people keep buying anyway. The next day the line is even longer. We raise the price again: today we sell the paper for $20. And there are even more people.

So we’ve landed in a self-fulfilling prophecy. The bank raises the price to make money. And people keep buying — also to make money. Because it’s true: prices really do get higher every day. You have to grab it while it’s cheaper! It gets to the point where banks sell the same piece of paper for $30, $40, $50 and even $100. And there are still no fewer people who want it. On the contrary, our actions only strengthen the belief that prices will keep rising.

While designers were repainting cars, banks were coming up with new prices. Over 6 years of trading like this, the prices of bonds and stocks grew by hundreds of percent.

We’re at the point where you, an ordinary person, and your neighbor John are trying to save your money. And here you also have a chance to multiply it. The value of the pieces of paper — and so of your assets — is growing.

At the same time, as a bank, you try to make more and more money from every piece of paper sold. That way your business can grow. And you’ll actually be supporting overall economic growth and strengthening the belief in it.

As a society, you all believe together in a bright, rich future. Imagine you grew up in abundance all your life. So did your partner. And you raised your child when the shelves were bursting with food, with shows, films, jazz, dancing and colorful ads everywhere. You recently bought a second car and a vacuum cleaner. All on credit, of course… so what. And it’s not just you who got lucky — the whole country has been living like this for about ten years. Besides, you’re all ordinary people, not anxious, mindful economists with a direct line to the cosmos. You don’t know that it could be any other way.

## And then

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-60.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={56} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Those very economists figured something out. In the fall of 1929, a small circle of people with great influence began to realize that stock prices didn’t match the real state of affairs. Because the value of stocks had stopped reflecting the growth of business profits and depended only on the amateurs in line at the bank. The minority of people who understood at least something about economics abruptly started selling their stocks, and that launched a wave of panic and dumping. That’s when you sell all the pieces of paper you’ve bought. After all, while the clever man with the mustache was buying stocks, I was buying too. And when he started selling them, I’ll sell too! Two fateful days in history follow.

On October 24, 1929, Black Thursday happens — a mass sell-off of stocks. This is when the first guys “with mustaches” start selling.

On October 29, 1929, Black Tuesday happens — the complete crash of the stock market. This is when it reaches you and your neighbor John.

If you had lived on that day, it would have looked like this. You woke up, had breakfast, drank your morning coffee. Got into your second car, bought on credit. You had barely walked into the office.

And the stocks of the whole country fell by 11% in a second. That’s only the beginning of the day. Then stock prices kept falling. In the end, every person on the street lost at least 40% of everything they had earned. In total, the country lost $30,000,000,000,000. This day will go down in history as Black Tuesday. That’s how the Great Depression began.

  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-61.avif'] },

<div class="ids__space M" />

## People lost everything

Banks went bankrupt one after another, because they didn’t have enough money to pay everyone who wanted to exchange their pieces of paper back for money. Remember, that price was pulled out of thin air. Banks simply didn’t hold the amount of money that was circulating in stocks across the country.

Not only ordinary people suffered, but the state too. It kept its money in the same banks that were going bankrupt every day.

Mass layoffs began. Every fifth person was left without a job. The crisis was so bad that cars were pulled by horses instead of running on gasoline, because there was no money for gas.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-63.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={59} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

People’s lives started to change. People cut their spending. The money in the wallet is running out, and it’s unclear when there will be more. → Prices in stores fell. People don’t buy as much pasta and sausages as before; they don’t have the money. So stores try to sell them at any price before they go bad. → Then companies’ revenue fell. Because of the sales in stores, you no longer earn as much on your pasta. You need to slow down production — and save money by laying people off. → People were left without wages, because production no longer needed so many hands. And it’s unclear what you’ll buy pasta and sausages with next month. No salary means you won’t go to the store. And around it goes.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-64.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={60} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

This is called a “deflationary spiral”. A complicated term, but we’ll need it right now.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Spinning the spiral

The government of the day tried to pull the country out of the crisis for 4 years. Well, they tried, they tried — why go on about it. It didn’t work out anyway. That’s not interesting to us.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-65.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={61} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Then, in 1933, a new president, Roosevelt, came to power. Here’s what he came up with. If we have a spiral that winds in one direction and creates a crisis, then to end the crisis it has to start unwinding in the other direction. Voilà! How do we do that?

For that, we open an economics textbook at the section “Keynesianism”. The school is called that simply because John Keynes came up with it. He suggested that the whole economy is built on one simple law: “one person’s spending is another person’s income”. This means all money is in endless circulation between people and companies. There can’t be any money that is just resting.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-66.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={62} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Now, if you take the deflationary spiral in one hand and Keynes’s law in the other, you can solve the crisis in the country and turn the spiral around. If there’s no spending → there’s no income. We need to make spending appear → then people will have income → then people will start spending money on themselves → then businesses will have income. This will help push the spiral in the opposite direction and bring the country back to the good old days.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-67.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={63} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Right, the plan is clear! Where do we start? Still unclear…

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Money! Money! Money!

Let’s print money!!

What other answer did you expect from the USA? With this money we’ll create government contracts. For example: build a road here. Paint a fence there. Sew new clothes. Make pasta. And we’ll pay your wages with the printed money. Then you’ll take that money to the store. The same money will reach the factories. And with the same money you’ll get your wages. An injection of printed money into society.

Hooray! Demand came back to the market. Now ordinary families’ spending grows → followed by prices in stores → then companies’ revenue grows → and finally, wages too! The spiral raced in the opposite direction.

And now the most attentive will notice an inconsistency: where’s the inflation? You can’t just print money! Let’s remember: who has all the gold at home after the First World War? And which country does Europe depend on for its imports? The crisis in America wasn’t bad only for America. The whole world depended on this crisis.

What’s more, Roosevelt abolished the gold standard!

The very law that, at the beginning of our story, actually made America such a rich country. Now the dollar was no longer backed by gold and didn’t depend on it.

Which means you can print as much money as you want. And is the American president a chicken or what? No, he’s not a chicken. He went ahead and printed plenty!

## 7 years of hunger

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-68.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={64} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

We just listen and don’t judge. Roosevelt’s solution really worked. In his very first 6 months in power, Roosevelt was able to show at least some positive results — namely, wages for ordinary people. For a country that had been sunk in a hopeless crisis for 4 years, this gave hope. Over the next 3 years, he brought US GDP back to its pre-crisis level. The country returned to its former life after 7 agonizing years.

Money gradually came back to people. And they hadn’t had time to forget how nice it was to buy a pile of new knick-knacks. To update everything that could be updated every year. The Great Depression sharpened the unmet need for endless consumption of goods. Now you have a whole country of poor souls whose favorite toy — spending money — was taken away from them for 7 years.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Why I spent your time on this

Since we’ve talked about the Great Depression for so long, it must really have influenced the development of designers. What changed?

First, the habit of consuming goods got stronger. This habit formed just like any other habit: through alternating two stages — the euphoria of getting a stimulus and the withdrawal of not getting it. Same in our story: at first there was a lot of money. Buy whatever you want! Then, in the crisis, there was suddenly catastrophically little. And finally the long-awaited chance to do it came back.

Second, technophilia appeared: the widespread love of technological progress and its results. When you can’t just open a can of food with your hands anymore — you absolutely have to use the Super Multi-tool 38-in-1, on sale for $9.99. The main stimulus for it was precisely that the government restored production and factories first of all — exactly to get out of the crisis.

And all of this adds up to

#### The fifth reason. People became convinced that a happy future lies at the machine tool

Of all professions and all possible ways to earn money, a machine at a factory was the most likely to provide for an ordinary family. So it helped bring money back to ordinary people.

At the same time, the machine at the factory also helped keep the money flowing back out. People’s demand had to be satisfied — given that it was still very profitable for all sides. Better yet, double the volume of production so you don’t end up in a crisis again.

Hard times create strong kittens. Yes, the history of design is directly linked to wars and crises. And we’ve just gone through one more.

Now we’re in an America that has only just beaten the Great Depression. People remember how good it was before. In stores, even in the niche of goodies and gadgets — when you’ve already stocked up on pasta and are picking a chocolate bar at the checkout — there was plenty of choice and competition. And credit covered all these desires. For several decades people have been drilled with the idea that this is where happiness, status and value lie. And we watch how society developed further with all this context.

And now it’s already the 1940s. A certain mustached guy is starting a new conflict in Europe. History repeats itself like during the First World War: one hemisphere fights while the other sponsors it. So the allies will once again come running to buy American goods. Which means once again you have to churn out everything that can be sold, not only for yourselves but also for buyers in Europe. In the First World War we were practicing; now we’re playing big!

So designers will be the first people they come to to get everything back on track. They’ll reinvent the same thing every year. Remember the first design department in history, which chose new colors every year and drew a new car body. That will be our solution! While engineers spend 5 or 10 years inventing improvements, designers will help us sell the same thing. For example, Touch ID appeared in the iPhone 5s in 2013, and the next step in security technology, Face ID, only appeared in 2017 in the iPhone X. Yet in 2014, 2015 and 2016 new, slightly redrawn iPhones came out anyway. In America this combination had been used since the 1920s, and in the 1940s they planned to go all in with it.

In the previous parts we learned 2 important points. First, new things should appear more often. Alfred Sloan, in part five, helped us figure out the first principle. Second, old things should become old faster. That’s the point we’ll look at now.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## A 180° turn of the wheel

Now we, as designers, have a clear goal: to lower the quality of things. To make them become obsolete faster, we need to understand how different materials behave. Throughout human history up to this point, people had done the opposite. We looked for the most durable material and ways to extend how long things could be used: technologies for processing wood and clay, different kinds of leather and textiles. Now we turn the wheel the other way.

Almost the whole production chain is ready. We know how: after the Great Depression we realized the future is at the assembly line. We know what: designers will come up with new shapes and silhouettes. But what do we make these silhouettes from in our factories? Now we need a new material for production. The green flags for the ideal candidate are roughly these:

- It can be stamped out (literally!)
- It’s safe for huge production volumes
- Predictable production quality and a low defect rate
- It wears out quickly
- It’s cheap

And people started looking for this material.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Worse, even worse!

Since most natural materials that both exist in large quantities and are easy to obtain had already been studied and tested over centuries, people knew exactly that they wouldn’t do. So they started shaking up chemists and their labs, trying to find the answer there — in materials that appear as a result of chemical reactions.

In their search, people first turned the corner with celluloid. They tried making various simple things out of it to test how the material would behave. Mostly glasses, small costume jewelry, trinkets. It didn’t work. The material was too fragile and demanding. Only one use was found for it: it really caught on as film for photo and movie cameras. Cheap cinema appeared, and after it, a chain of movie theaters all over America. All of humanity got a new and cheap form of entertainment. But that’s not what we were looking for. Let’s move on.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-70.avif'] },
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-71.avif'] },

<div class="ids__space S" />

Then they ended up on Bakelite street. Leo Baekeland invented it back in 1907, but at the time nobody appreciated it. The material is brown and looks like wood. Why would we, at the beginning of the 20th century, need your artificial Bakelite chest of drawers when we can buy one made of real wood? They didn’t know yet :) what they were getting into. So Bakelite was seen as an alternative: a way to replace expensive wood with this cheap polymer. Closer to the 1940s, people remembered it and started taking it more seriously. Bakelite was used to make boxes, clock and radio cases, lamp bases — in short, interior items and appliances. Bakelite could be made translucent or dyed in different colors, and then it looked like amber, coral, jade or malachite. Dyed Bakelite was used for cigarette holders, jewelry and clutch bags.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-83.avif'] },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={68} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

Here, let’s remember what happened in America during the First World War. A whole new section of goods appeared on store shelves: household appliances. All of it runs on electricity. Oh, miracle! Bakelite turns out to be suitable for that.

So why don’t we live in a world of Bakelite? Bakelite was the first shot in the right direction, so it was seen more positively than it really deserved. And there were many problems. First, the production cost. Second, how complex and toxic production was. Third, its appearance could have been better: like celluloid, Bakelite stayed fragile. On top of that, the color range was limited because of its natural brown tint. It’s hard to cover such a rich warm color with a cold dye without getting a muddy final shade.

## Plastic!

Plastic became the main trend. Not that it was some unique material — it just didn’t have the problems its competitors had. It was simply normal. And that helped it win. First, it’s truly cheap to produce. Second, it finally solved the problem of “good enough to buy but not good enough to use”. So in the store, plastic containers, lamps and plates made the impression of sturdy things, but within a few months defects, scuffs and breakages appeared, and the flawless look was gone as if on a timer.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Propaganda

As soon as the benefits of plastic became obvious to manufacturers, companies started selling the idea to society. And society didn’t mind.

Plastic impressed everyone. For example, from April 22 to 27, 1946, a Plastics Exhibition was held. 20,000 people visited it on the very first day. It showed disposable and reusable tableware, dolls, lamps, electronics, furniture, suitcases, accessories and a ton of other things. 2 things made the biggest impression.

The first was transparent food packaging. It revolutionized the shopping experience in grocery stores. Before that, our arsenal had metal cans, glass jars and paper. Of these, only glass let you see the product itself. But I think you understand that glass jars are expensive and not every product can be packed in them. Now the buyer could judge the quality of the food right at the shelf and choose what they liked best.

The second was bulky, solid things, such as suitcases and furniture. Before, such things were associated with a craftsman’s long, careful work — and, because of the complexity, the quality of the materials and the cost of making them, with a high price.

Humanity is saved at last!

Now we have a simple and cheap way to meet most of humanity’s needs. We don’t have to abuse the planet with heavy industry and burning through natural resources. Plastic will save the environment and the whole planet from the consequences of production (how does it feel to read these lines in 2025, when we already know where it all led?)

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-82.avif'] },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={72} />

<div class="ids__space S" />

To fuel demand for plastic goods, another series of exhibitions was launched. They were organized by Edgar Kaufmann Jr. and called “Useful Objects under $5 / $10”. Another shift in the system happened: instead of high art, galleries showed plastic knick-knacks. And that was also part of the propaganda. Because your attitude to the same kettle will be different. It all depends on the first impression. You can see a kettle on a white pedestal in perfect light inside a spacious, trendy gallery. Or in an ordinary discount store, on a smelly bottom shelf, with dust and a torn box. And even if you were going to buy the kettle at the discount store anyway, your choice will be largely shaped by that first impression. Even in the 21st century, with all our banner blindness, this still happens from time to time. Never mind the middle of the 20th century, when all this was only beginning.

Another example of plastic propaganda: they tried to use it at iconic events and casually remind people, “by the way, this is plastic too”. For example, the flag Neil Armstrong planted on the Moon was made of nylon, which is also a kind of plastic. Well, if Neil Armstrong himself! On the Moon!! With the American flag!!! Made of plastic!!!! Then why should I be any worse? That’s how the generation of our grandmothers and great-grandmothers bought the idea of this cheap polymer.

  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-78.avif'], size: 'XL' },

<div class="ids__space M" />

## Artists went to the factory

Designers’ knowledge and skills lose their value in an instant. Let’s recall all the context we got in the first parts of this article. Now all the guns we hung up earlier are going to fire.

What was a designer’s main quality at the beginning of our story? The ability to make everything by hand! Understanding the specifics of a material, the different ways of working with it, and endless hours spent polishing your skill and building processes at the manufactory.

Now, on the one hand, the whole process of making things happened on the assembly line. On the other, one object began to use many different materials: plastic and other polymers, various metal alloys. The variety of machine tools could already handle any request — faster, better and more predictably than a person. So, comrades, to the factory!

Designers no longer need to know the specifics of a single material, because we stopped working with materials. We no longer had to work with our hands. Our skills are no longer needed.

Instead, we sit and fantasize! And whatever we come up with, we draw! The guys will come, take it to the factory and figure out how to build it without us.

In the new reality, the more important knowledge became the specifics of factory production, the behavior of synthetic materials, marketing, behaviorism and the psychology of influence.

<div class="ids__space M" />

## The randomizer switches on

Globally, the result of our work becomes less predictable.

Up to this point in history, every design object had the qualities of its era. What’s more, it tried to keep them up to preserve a single style of the era. That is, all the teacups from the 1850s look alike. You can trace trends and common tendencies in them: roses, frills, shape, colors. That was the designer’s task: to make a new piece in an already existing style.

From the 1950s and 60s, the rules of the game change. Now the task is to make something that has never existed anywhere. A new car looking similar to a competitor’s car that came out a couple of months ago is a death sentence. Because there’s more and more choice, and people start choosing by unique, rough-edged features in order to stand out.

Design loses its lineage. Historical styles dissolve. Designers start trying lots of provocative visual solutions. Whatever hits the mark, whatever people vote for with their money, becomes the new trend.

  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-84.avif'] },
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-75.avif'] },

## Demand for designers appeared

Didn’t it seem to you too that this approach to industry and to making things and ads sounds like a looot of working hands? To keep the whole consumption machine running.

Yes, to me too.

Demand for designers appeared because we were the only ones who could adapt to any new reality in time. We figured out how to manage capitalism, competition and human attention at the same time.

<div class="ids__space XL" />

<p class="loud">Part 8. How is General Motors doing?</p>

I have one last gun left in my pocket for you. Let’s remember GM one last time. They became the most valuable company in the world, made half of all the cars sold in the US, and held on to their greatness right up until 2008.

And remember the first design department in history, made up of the 50 first designers with whom it all started in 1923? By the mid-1960s, 1,600 people worked there, including car and industrial designers, engineers, sculptors, color and fabric specialists, draftsmen, wood and metal model makers, and sketch artists. At the time it was the largest design department in the car industry.

<Gallery figures={[
  { image: images['./how-did-designers-come-about-85.avif'], size: 'XL' },
]} title={frontmatter.title} lang={lang} context="note" offset={77} />

<div class="ids__space M" />

But in fact that’s not much of an achievement. You can hire any number of people. Then fire them. And nothing will happen to you. And nothing will change globally. Because it’s all just people rotating between jobs and the economic cycles of society. The number of jobs says something about design’s influence on the world only indirectly.

But here’s a fact that really confirms the influence design has on the world.

In about 10 years of work, from the mid-1920s, Alfred Sloan and his design department managed to change public opinion globally. Buying something was no longer seen as an investment in a good thing. Quality and durability faded into the background. By 1934, people were changing their cars much more often — every 5 years. Although, of course, the cars could have lasted twice as long, thanks to the work of engineers, designers and safety specialists. But every 5 years people still demanded something new.

The ice had broken.

And by 1955 this period had shrunk even further. To — attention — two years! In just 20 years, the habit of consumption sped up more than twice. Meanwhile, nobody stopped the work of the same engineers, designers and safety specialists. Cars became more reliable, sturdier, faster and safer — in a word, they could last longer than in the 1930s. But the period of use was shrinking instead! People kept changing their cars more and more often.

Designers beat the engineers.

People got stable jobs — and, along with them, a dependence on new knick-knacks. Businesses got money from the uninterrupted sales of those very knick-knacks — and an endless competitive race. And the world got beauty, progress and technology — and, along with them, the problems of ecology and overproduction.

## It’s all just beginning

Let’s sum up the path we’ve traveled over the last century and a half. We started as a group of 10 craftsmen in a cramped manufactory, and now designers are:

- A complex set of skills and working tools that went far beyond craft and touched even economics, psychology and propaganda
- Daily experiments. We’ve become freer in what we can offer society, because the crowd asks for spectacle and waits for emotions and novelty
- The engine of the future. We’ve learned to push visual culture forward and decide what the world and its future should look like

It all began with repainting cars and led to what we see now. Yes, our story ends in the 1960s, and in a way that’s unfair, because a lot happened between 1960 and 2025 too. Computers and neural networks alone are worth a lot.

But the further development of design happened because the demand had already been formed. It had become clear who would use design and how, and what problems designers could help solve. I wanted to show you exactly the part of the story where designers were feeling their way, when this demand was only just being born. Every hero of this story trod a path for our work today. Now you know where so many designers came from, and why we are in such demand.
