<p class="loud">— Why do some designers get cool clients, and others get only losers?!</p>

You scroll through someone’s portfolio and think: “This is really cool! Look at those clients — big, major ones! Now that’s a cool designer. I wish I could be like that!!”

In reality, the size of the client has nothing to do with how cool a project is. A clear example is Art. Lebedev Studio. Yes, huge corporations and whole cities come to them — but the logos that went viral were the ones for John Fyodor and Khovansky. Just some burger place, just some blogger.

The level of the client only affects the designer’s ego — provided that ego has nothing else to plug it with except the line *“Actually, I’ve worked with…”*. The only client-related parameter that really affects a project is how much you like each other as people. And even that is optional: without love you can run projects steadily on skills and well-built processes. It’s just nicer with love. And simpler.

So why are the cool ones over there and the losers over here? And how come every time the cool ones show up, nobody invites you? The truth is:

<div class="ids__space XL"></div>

The same people come to everyone

<div class="ids__space XL"></div>

The same tasks come to everyone

<div class="ids__space XL"></div>

All that matters is what happens next

<div class="ids__space XL"></div>

In fact, the people “with cool clients” — you already know that’s a fiction, but let’s call them that — know the answer to one important question very well: what would we enjoy doing right now? They whisper, dig around, scout. And between “job done” and “time to have fun” there is one more need: “enjoying doing it”.

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

What can that need consist of? For the designer:

- Showing something new and interesting, maybe beyond the usual
- Trying a new technique or style, stealing something really beautiful from something they once saw somewhere
- Rethinking old experience and bringing in a new context. Or flying off into the future entirely

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

For the client:

- Growing their business steadily, without jerks to the right or left (everything around is already on fire and breaking — can the designer at least not play around in the sandbox?!)
- Being heard. Getting enough respect as one of the few sources of truth for this particular business
- Getting a solution to the problem they came with

Admiration for both the project and the portfolio happens when the designer manages to meet their own needs in the project along with the client’s. You must do everything you were asked to do — that’s what you’re paid for. But not only that. And not only that way.

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

Ignoring the client’s needs completely won’t work. Well, it will work a couple of times. Then you’ll have to close shop, because the old clients ran away, new ones come reluctantly, and the money runs out.

Ignoring your own needs completely is easier — but then…

<p class="loud">— Why do other designers get fucking awesome clients, and I get only losers!</p>
