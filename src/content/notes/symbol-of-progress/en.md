Remember the Greeks, the Romans and similar guys in robes. They wrote with whatever was lying on the ground: stones, twigs… And the Greek alphabet is built so that all its glyphs — the letters — are written from left to right.

Why?

A stone slab doesn’t come with Ctrl+Z. Every stroke has to be right the first time, and instead of an Apple Pencil you have a chisel and a hammer.

These are the physical limits of writing. We write from left to right, so every letter can start where the previous one ends (taking sidebearings into account). How do you measure the space for a letter written from right to left if you don’t know how much room it will take? Measure too much and you get an extra gap; measure too little and it won’t fit.

The Greek alphabet is ideal for those who write from left to right: it was created from the start to set large walls of text with as few mistakes and as little waste as possible, at a time when a spelling, punctuation or aesthetic mistake was costly.

The Cyrillic letter “Я” (ya), which looks like a mirrored R, doesn’t fit into this system. Try an experiment, in your head or, better, on a sheet of paper. Start writing the letters “M”, “B”, “K”. Each letter starts on the left, and the next one joins the previous one nicely. If we need mathematical precision, we can build a grid, quickly measure the width of the sidebearings and make the setting uniform.

Now what if we write the letter “Я” in this perfect system? Say we’ve measured the sidebearing. What next? How far do we step back? By eye? And do I trust my eye enough to measure the perfect distance for the letter?

Letters aren’t just a set of sticks. They are a source of information and a way to pass it on, a purely practical thing. So a new system was needed. First, with the lowest possible chance of error, so it’s easy to use. Second, built on the simple technologies of its time, so it’s easy to reproduce.

And once such a system took shape, humanity started to scale fast. Stones and twigs gave way to quills, paper… We stopped depending on the tool we wrote with. And this is where import substitution, so familiar to us, enters the story.

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

In the 18th century, Peter the Great approved the shape of the letter “Я” we know today, written from right to left. By then, stone slabs, birch bark and clay tablets were long forgotten, and quills were the new norm. For a quill, moving in any direction is no problem.

You can scribble the whole alphabet without any limits on how your hand moves. That’s what the Chinese did, for example: think of their characters — their writing system assumed free movement of the hand from the very beginning, and it even gave birth to the art of calligraphy.

If humanity hadn’t chosen globalization and had stayed Greeks in robes, we would never have thought of flipping the “R” to make “Я”.

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

The fact that we can write the letter “Я” has become, for me, a symbol of progress. It motivates me to be more curious about everything new and to look at the old in new ways. What if it has already become a vestige?
